Light is slow here

In this park, the speed of light is 5 kilometers per hour — a brisk walk. Just like in the real world, the speed of light is not attainable, only approachable asymptotically. The more you speed up, the more relativistic effects will become apparent: length contraction, time dilation, Terrell rotation, etc. The Doppler effect of light also becomes apparent, with objects in front of you blueshifted, objects behind you redshifted, and transverse Doppler effects visible on objects in motion.

Field guide

Everything you see is computed by tracing light back down your past light cone and boosting it into your moving frame. This is the same arithmetic that applies to starlight, just with c reduced to walking pace, so it can be observed more easily on human scales.

Things to try

  • Accelerate by pressing the Up arrow. Your field of view begins to condense in the direction you are traveling, and becomes blueshifted. If you look behind you by using your mouse to turn, that field of view is redshifted and stretched out. This is relativistic aberration.
  • The beacons all flash once per second of world time. Walk toward one and the flashing speeds up; walk away and it slows down. Also observe the timers labeled "Your watch" and "World clock", and see how they begin to differ.
  • Two rulers lie in the park, both 60 m, banded red and white every 2 m. One runs along the corridor, one across it. When traveling down the corridor at speed, observe the contraction of one ruler relative to the other.
  • Stand still and watch the shuttle run back and forth. It becomes shorter when it is moving the fastest. And because light from its far end left earlier than light from its near end, it also looks turned. That apparent twist is Terrell rotation.
  • The carousel cars are contracted by their own motion, and you see each one at a different moment in its trip around the ring. The half of the ring running away from you is beamed almost to black while the half coming toward you flares: the relativistic searchlight effect.

A note on Doppler shift

The rendering of blueshifts and redshifts is, of course, an approximation, especially at extreme blueshift levels. Each surface color is treated as a reflectance spectrum under a 5800 K sun, shifted by the Doppler factor, and re-integrated against the CIE 1931 color matching functions. At extreme redshifts, the color fades to black. And at extreme blueshifts (past violet) the color turns to a metallic silver to represent x-rays, then to bright white to represent gamma rays (obviously unphysical, but just for a sense of it).
